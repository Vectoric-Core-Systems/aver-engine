Fluids

Fluids, weighed and thickened

Aver.Water and Aver.Render.Fluid merged into one module, Aver.Fluids. A volume is authored in two real-world numbers — density in kg/m³, viscosity in Pa·s — over a pressurised soft-body shell, beside an analytic Gerstner surface and its underwater fog override. One of those two numbers is physics. The other is a fit, and the module says so rather than pretending otherwise.

One module, three things — and one it refuses

Aver.Water and Aver.Render.Fluid merged into a single module, Aver.Fluids (b887a78), on a reasoning its own CMakeLists.txt states plainly: the old split “described the implementation rather than the domain” — an author placing a WATER record does not care whether the surface they get is evaluated in a vertex shader or stepped by a solver, and the two already shared a format record, a composition-root call site, and a set of colours.

What the merged module actually holds is three things. The surface (GerstnerWave.hpp, WaterRenderer) is Gerstner-wave math with zero knowledge of rendering — no rhi::, no device — drawn as a depth-tested, alpha-blended grid that recentres under the camera without ever re-uploading its geometry. Underwater (Underwater.hpp) is a pure function that swaps an authored rhi::SkyAtmosphere’s six fog fields for underwater ones as the camera crosses the surface, blended across a fade band so the swap doesn’t pop. The volume (FluidVolume.hpp, FluidScene) is the soft-body half: a seed shell built by pure arithmetic goes out to the physics solver, simulated vertices come back and get redrawn.

What it does not hold, on purpose, is buoyancy. modules/physics/src/Buoyancy.cpp owns that, and the module’s own README states the rule directly: it “must never learn what Jolt is,” and the two halves “meet in the composition root and nowhere else.” The physics dependency is scoped the same way at build time — FluidScene.cpp, the one file that touches the solver’s ABI, is only added to the target when AVER_MODULE_PHYSICS is on; without it, the analytic surface, the underwater override and the seed-shell arithmetic still build, and the module quietly loses only the ability to simulate.

Density is real. Viscosity is a fit.

A fluid volume can be authored with two solver knobs replaced by two real-world numbers — densityKgM3 and viscosityPaS, on a FluidPhysicsMaterial — and the honest thing about that pair is that the two halves are not the same kind of number, and the source says so at length rather than papering over it.

Density is real, not a fit. It becomes an actual per-particle mass (fluidParticleMassKg = density × enclosed volume ÷ particle count), and the solver honours that mass directly through each particle’s inverse mass. A denser fluid genuinely has more inertia and sags harder under the same pressure and compliance — checkable, and checked (FluidVolumeTest::testDensityScalesMassAndPressure).

Viscosity is a calibrated fit, not real. Jolt’s soft-body solver has no shear-stress term at all; the closest proxy it has is per-vertex linear damping, and damping removes energy uniformly rather than in proportion to shear. fluidDampingForViscosity maps one onto the other, and the mapping comes from a measurement, not a derivation: FluidDampingCalibrationTest.cpp spawns the FirstPerson pool’s own shell (300×200×60 cm, 8×8×4, 258 particles) at production compliance and pressure, settles it three seconds, hits every particle with the same 400 cm/s lateral impulse at once, steps 600 times at 1/60s, and fits mean per-vertex speed to v(t) = v0 · e^(-t/τ). τ falls from 2.84s at damping 0.01 to a minimum of 0.63s at damping 3.0, which is why the mapping’s two anchors are kDampingAnchorLow = 0.01 and kDampingAnchorHigh = 3.0, not 10. Past 3.0 the trace stops being monotonic — the damping=10 row shows a genuine second slosh mode (speed falls to 3.4 cm/s by t=0.35s, then rises back to 9.4 cm/s at t=2.18s before finally dying), which is exactly why that row has the sweep’s worst fit (r²=0.618): a single exponential is a weaker fit precisely where a second mode is visible. The source calls this “a real finding, not a fit error,” and says the reliable claim is the direction — lighter damping settles slower than heavier, end to end — not a monotonic curve across all seven rows. So the mapping is a log-log interpolation between exactly the two anchors kViscosityAnchorLowPaS (water, 1.0×10-3 Pa·s) and kViscosityAnchorHighPaS (10 Pa·s, the top of the design brief’s own honey range), clamped rather than extrapolated past either one. A viscosity above 10 Pa·s — lava’s own 102–104 Pa·s — returns the identical damping as 10 Pa·s exactly. FluidPhysicsMaterial::Lava() is real density (a genuinely heavier sag) paired with a damping response presently indistinguishable from honey’s — an acknowledged gap the comment records rather than covers with an invented formula reaching past what was ever measured.

Two ways to author one, one place that resolves it

A fluid volume can come from a level’s own WATER record (SandboxApp::applyLevelWater, simulated when it names simulate) or from a class graph’s COMP <id> Fluid line (GraphComponentTree.ApplyFluid). Both build a FluidVolumeDesc and both end at the identical function, fluids::fluidResolvePhysicsMaterial, called from FluidScene::spawn “and only from there” — the one point every fluid request converges on regardless of how it was authored, so the precedence rule below cannot be forgotten by adding a third way in later.

A COMP ... Fluid line is a solver request, not an ECS presence — fluids::FluidScene owns no entity and reads no CWorld at all. The placeholder entity the record creates still exists and sits where it says (so an inspector can find it), but nothing the solver reads ever comes from that entity. pos= is read as an absolute world centre, not a parent-relative offset, for the same reason a WATER record’s centre already is: a true hierarchical placement would need this component’s own world transform, which does not exist yet at the point the kind is applied. scale= multiplies FluidVolumeDesc’s own default half-extent (100, 100, 50 cm), so scale=1,1,1 spawns exactly the pool an unauthored record already would.

a class graph’s own COMP line
COMP Pool Fluid pos=0,0,60 scale=3,2,1.2 preset=water

A level’s own WATER ... simulate record went through a real startup bug worth recording rather than a clean design from the start: applyLevelWater runs during onInit, before the render features that back FluidScene exist, so FluidScene::spawn’s own if (!ready_) return 0 silently refused every level-authored pool at startup, logged only as “the fluid body could not be created.” The fix is a latch, not a reorder — loadLevel is also reached from File > Open Level and the project browser, both mid-frame with a command list already open — so applyLevelWater now records the desired desc and a single drain in onUpdate performs the actual spawn once the frame loop turns.

A material and a hand-set damping can’t both win

fluidResolvePhysicsMaterial enforces one rule, in the one place that can never forget it: if a desc names a material and its raw damping field has already been changed away from the engine’s own default, the call refuses the entire spawn rather than silently picking a winner — it returns false, leaves the desc untouched, and (when asked) writes a message naming both the material’s implied damping and the conflicting raw value, so whatever called it can log which one an author probably meant.

The check is against the default damping constant, not a separate “was this authored” flag — FluidVolumeDesc carries none — the identical sentinel-by-default-value convention its density and pressure fields already use for the same reason. A desc that never names a material is untouched by any of this: every caller from before the material layer existed spawns exactly as it did before.

Water is a material now, not a renderer that happens to draw water

FluidScene::transparentPass is deleted. A live volume now draws through setDrawBinding + setDrawBlended + drawMesh with an authored .ocmat, exactly like every other translucent mesh in a level — in the ray-tracing structure, sorted back-to-front against glass, shadowed by the sun, fogged, and shaded by the one material path everything else already uses. A WATER record gains its own material <name> token (OcWaterPlacement::material) to say which .ocmat, kept deliberately apart from the preset/density/viscosity trio above, which stay FluidPhysicsMaterial’s own — two floats that become particle mass and Jolt damping, carrying no colour, no roughness, no IOR, and never have. Naming a preset “honey” has never made anything look like honey; the look was a pair of compile-time literals in a fluid shader no level could reach, until this release gave it an actual .ocmat to reach for instead.

an .ocmap’s own WATER record, both materials named
WATER name Pool level 60 bounds -300 -200 300 200 simulate preset water material M_Water

Being in the ray-tracing structure is load-bearing, not a bonus: the volume-absorption ray needs a back face to find, and a water surface only reaches that path by being an ordinary raytraced mesh. Being drawn as one also gives AVER_MAT_TWO_SIDED its first reader on the blended lane — the scene’s own cull mode is None, so a closed, single-sided volume used to composite every face it owns; the blended branch now discards back faces when a material says twosided=0. Measured on the pool: 5.95% of the viewport changes; M_Glass, which deliberately sets twosided=1 “because you walk round it,” is bit-identical.

Two defects surfaced by measuring the result rather than by reading the diff. The draw was gated on !hideEditorScene, copied from a placeholder loop above it — that flag means “a real level is loaded,” so the water vanished in exactly the case that matters and nowhere else; the render came back bit-identical to no water at all, which is what caught it. Separately, and predating this change: the blended flush sorts back-to-front on the draw’s own world-space translation, and the fluid buffer was drawn with an identity matrix, so a pool authored away from the origin still claimed to sit at world (0,0,0) and could composite on the wrong side of a pane it was plainly in front of. packFluidVerts now subtracts the volume’s own centre and the draw supplies the real translation — the same local frame the soft-body vertex packer already used, for the same reason.

Cost, stated plainly: 28.8 → 40.4 ms at one measured overhead pool camera — the water is 11.6 ms there now, because it pays for ray-traced shadows at four rays per pixel, cone-traced GI, fog, and a thickness ray the old bespoke shader never paid for anything of. A scene with no water (ElectricDreams) is unchanged. Absorption, refraction, total internal reflection and caustics all reach water only because it takes this path now — see rendering.html's own account of the optics for the detail.

What’s left of FluidScene is what its own header always said it modelled itself on — a soft-body scene: spawn, update, prePass, drawHandle. It produces the geometry; the renderer shades it. 481 lines of the parallel render stack this replaced — a dedicated vertex/pixel shader pair, its own pipeline object, and the baked target-format tracking that kept it in step with the scene target — came out in a separate, reviewable removal once the new path was verified bit-identical on a still frame. The Gerstner ocean surface is untouched by any of this: it’s a vertex-displaced analytic sheet with no mesh, so it can’t simply become a mesh with a material, and stays its own decision — its tick() still has no caller, unchanged from before.

Pressure can’t be a constant

Jolt turns a soft body’s pressure coefficient into a per-face impulse of pressure · dt / V · area, so what a particle actually feels scales with its own share of surface area divided by the enclosed volume — and both of those change with a pool’s size and its subdivision. One constant cannot be right for two different pools: the source’s own account is that a coefficient tuned for a bathtub was “about a millionth” of what a 6×4×1.2 m pool needed, and that pool collapsed into a puddle on the basin floor within two seconds.

So fluidPressureFor derives it instead, from a balance over the whole top face — pressure × area = gravity × the mass of that face’s own particles — counted over totals rather than a per-particle cell, because a rim particle owns less area than an interior one but carries the identical mass. The balance assumes pressure alone holds the top face up, which is an over-estimate (the constraint network running down the walls also carries some of that load), so a fitted headroom fraction, kFluidPressureHeadroom = 0.6, scales the derived number down. That 0.6 is measured, not argued — swept 0.0 to 1.05 on the FirstPerson pool at 8×8×4 for four seconds, final depth as a fraction of where it started:

final depth84%86%95%94%97%97%134% (ballooning)

0.4–0.75 holds the shape; past that it inflates. 0.6 sits in the middle of that plateau. The conversion from the engine’s own centimetres to Jolt’s metres is its own hazard here, and a real one: kFluidCmToJolt is a compound factor (gravity loses a factor of 100, volume 106, area 104) specific to this one pressure ratio, and is explicitly not interchangeable with the plain volume conversion the mass formula uses. Getting it wrong once already did real damage: a coefficient 10,000× too large “turned a 1.2 m deep pool into a balloon that swallowed the camera” inside four seconds — not a hypothetical, a bug this exact module shipped and then fixed.

The editor’s preview does not simulate

Opening a class graph with a Fluid component shows its shape in the Viewport tab — but what draws is generateFluidSeedShell called directly, pure arithmetic over the desc, with no solver and no device involved. That is a deliberate limit, not a shortcut the editor happens to take: the solver only advances inside aver_phys_step, whose one non-test call site is gated on Play or a non-empty --spawn-test class, so in edit mode it never runs at all. A preview that asked the solver for a shape would show a frozen one and quietly imply the fluid does not move.

Stepping a physics world just for a preview was considered and rejected — it would need a physics world the editor does not otherwise run, to animate a shape the engine’s own lifecycle notes already record as tested at zero (“no play session ⇒ step count 0”). The seed shell is drawn through the same component-transform matrix every other placeholder uses, unlike the runtime path (which draws the simulated buffer at an identity matrix, because the solver already returns world-space positions) — the seed shell spans local space about its own centre, so the ordinary per-component transform is the correct one to place it with.

It still collapses, at its own calibrated proportions

Exactly one test in this tree steps the real Jolt solver and checks that pressure holds a shell up — SoftBodyTest::testPressureHoldsAShellUp — and it passes: at the FirstPerson pool’s own 6×4×1.2 m, 8×8×4 proportions, a pressurised shell keeps more than 90% of its depth after four seconds. But look at what it hands the solver: aver_phys_softbody_create(..., nullptr, ...) — a null invMasses array, which is Jolt’s own implicit mass of 1.0 for every particle. That is the pre-density behaviour, not water.

A pool built with FluidPhysicsMaterial::Water() at those identical proportions gets a real particle mass instead: 998 kg/m³ × 28.8 m³ ÷ 258 particles ≈ 111 kg per particle — about 111× heavier than the only mass this solver has ever been proven, by an actual physics step, to hold up. fluidPressureFor does scale the pressure coefficient by that same real mass, and FluidVolumeTest::testDensityScalesMassAndPressure confirms the arithmetic does what the derivation says. But that test never calls aver_phys_softbody_create at all — it checks that the formula’s output number scales correctly, not that Jolt’s own constraint solver still balances a shell built from real, non-unit particle masses the way the linear balance in the derivation assumes it does.

That gap is real and, as far as this tree’s own test suite goes, currently unclosed: nothing here steps the solver with a non-unit density and checks the outcome. It matches what a live-editor investigation in this same working session found by hand — a preset=water volume at the identical calibrated proportions, confirmed to be genuinely simulating (its mesh kept changing through a play session, not sitting static), settling into a shallow puddle rather than holding a pool’s depth. The pressure derivation is sound arithmetic and is checked as arithmetic; what is not yet checked is whether Jolt’s actual solver response is mass-invariant the way that derivation assumes. That is the next thing to test here, not something this tree has verified yet.

Three traps in the solver underneath

All three surfaced only once this module became the first caller ever to hand Jolt’s soft-body solver a non-zero pressure, and all three are worth knowing before touching the shell generator or the buoyancy path again.

Inward winding silences pressure, with no warning at all. Jolt’s own ApplyPressure opens with if (six_volume > 0.0f) — an inside-out shell gets no pressure whatsoever, no error, no log line. The engine’s own winding fix (3ae1e3a) turned out to be a double negative: buildSoftShared used to swap two indices per face on the theory that Jolt’s own axis conversion mirrors the mesh, which is true, but this engine’s winding convention is already the opposite hand from the one Jolt’s volume integral assumes — so the mirror alone was already correct, and the extra swap undid it. generateFluidSeedShell’s own per-face winding is, by its own comment, “not been checked by running anything”: a wrong tangent-pair entry would still leave the shell closed (vertex sharing doesn’t depend on winding), with exactly one face turned inside out — precisely the defect only a per-face winding check, not a closedness check, would ever catch.

Buoyancy hard-asserts on a soft body. Body::ApplyBuoyancyImpulse opens with JPH_ASSERT(IsRigidBody()). A fluid volume sits below its own water plane by construction — fully submerged from the first step — so asking it to float is fatal, not merely wrong. Buoyancy.cpp now explicitly skips non-rigid bodies on both the per-body and the global water-plane paths.

Pressure must be derived in metres, not the engine’s own centimetres. Jolt integrates in metres everywhere; the engine authors everything else in centimetres. kFluidCmToJolt is a compound conversion for this one specific ratio (gravity × volume ÷ area) and is not interchangeable with the plain cm3→m3 factor the mass formula uses — conflating the two, or dropping the conversion, is “not subtle in one direction and invisible in the other,” per the module’s own words on the balloon it once produced.

What this does not do

  • Cannot splash or merge. A simulated volume is a fixed-topology shell — its vertex count and the edges between them are frozen the instant aver_phys_softbody_create builds it. No parameter can make it tear, join, or reflow, so none is offered; anything that reads as water breaking apart or joining needs an actual particle system, not this solver.
  • No soft shoreline. The Gerstner surface is depth-tested but never samples scene depth, so it meets sloped terrain in a hard line rather than a fade — deferred rather than half-built, because IDevice::sceneDepthTexture() returns 0 on every backend but D3D12 today.
  • The ocean’s own wave clock has never been advanced. WaterRenderer::tick(dtSeconds) exists to drive its Gerstner phase forward every frame, but grepping SandboxApp.cpp turns up zero call sites for it — so an analytic ocean’s own chop has stood frozen at t=0 since the surface first shipped. A simulated volume’s own surface ripple, by contrast, genuinely animates: its clock is threaded through every frame via FluidScene::update(elapsedSeconds).
  • Only the first WATER record in a level ever renders. The format allows several — an ocean and a pool in the same level — but applyLevelWater draws exactly the first and warns about the rest; that is a gap in this one consumer, not in what the format can carry.
  • Correction: a simulated volume is no longer invisible to shadows, GI or ray tracing. FluidScene::transparentPass is deleted; a volume now draws through the ordinary drawMesh path with an authored .ocmat, exactly like every other translucent mesh, which puts it in the shadow cascades, the ray-tracing TLAS and GI voxelisation for the first time — see “Water is a material now” below for the rewrite and what it costs. This bullet described the previous release; it does not describe this one.
  • Vulkan is untested here, not confirmed broken. The module is written against the generic rhi:: vocabulary and should compile, but the post chain the surface would composite through is refused on Vulkan today, so no claim is made about the final image on that backend.
  • The underwater fog numbers are placeholders. Every field on UnderwaterFogTuning is an unvalidated starting point — there is no furnace- or SH-integral-style oracle for “looks murky” the way the sky and GI code got one, and the existing fog machinery it reuses was tuned only at kilometre-scale outdoor distances, a genuinely different regime from a few metres of underwater visibility.
  • It still collapses at its own calibrated proportions — see above. That one is not cosmetic.
